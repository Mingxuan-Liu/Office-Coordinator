How your desk gets chosen

You rank desks. A solver seats everyone at once. This page says what it is optimising, why the answer it returns is provably the best one available, and what it cannot do. Every number below is computed in your browser from this year’s published configuration — nothing here is a stock illustration.

The short version

  • Speed buys nothing. When you submit is not an input. First minute or last, same answer.
  • Rank honestly, in the order you actually want. The order is the only signal you can send, and it is the only one used.
  • You will get one of the … desks you asked for — or the run fails loudly and a human comes back to you. There is no path where you silently end up somewhere you never ranked.

Why ranking beats racing

The previous process was a shared document that opened at an announced time. Everyone piled in at once and typed their name next to a desk. Whoever’s laptop woke up first got the window.

That process was optimising something. Just not preference. It rewarded being awake, being on a fast connection, and not being mid-observing-run in Chile. It also quietly punished caring: if the one desk you actually wanted was gone in the first four seconds, you were left choosing between options you were indifferent about, with no way to say so.

Here, submission time is not an input to the answer. Not as a tie-break, not as a tie-break of a tie-break. The only thing timestamps are used for is deciding which of your own submissions is the latest. Submit in the first minute or the last; change your mind six times; the result is identical. The solver is a pure function of (responses, config, seed) — same inputs, byte-identical output file, on any machine.

What replaces the race is an ordering. You say which desks you want and in what order. Everyone else does the same. Then one calculation seats everybody.

Everyone is scored on the same curve. There is no per-person weighting, no intensity field, nothing to inflate. Getting your first choice is worth …; your last is worth ….

The solver returns the seating that makes the sum of everyone’s points as large as it can possibly be. That sentence does more work than it looks like it does, because what you lose by being moved off a desk depends on how far down your list the next available desk sits — and that differs enormously between people.

Two people. Both put the same desk first. Only one can have it. The question the solver asks is not who wants it more — the curve gives both of them … for a first choice, so “wanting it more” is not something this form can express. The question is what it costs to move each of them off it.

Note what did not decide this: not who submitted first, not seniority, not who wrote a more emphatic justification. The contested desk goes to the person whose alternatives are worse. Move the highlighted row so both people have the same fallback and watch the two totals become exactly equal — that is the one and only situation where the published seed does anything, and it is dealt with further down.

A global optimum, not a sequence of local decisions

There is an obvious way to do this, and it is wrong. Take everyone in some order; give the first person their best available desk, then the second, and so on. That is serial dictatorship, and it is exactly what the shared document was — the order was just “who clicked first”.

It is fast, it is easy to explain, and it gives a different answer depending on the order — sometimes a much worse one. Below is a small example with … people and … desks. All … pick orders are run out, then compared against the answer this system gives.

Every greedy row above is a sequence of decisions that each looked fine at the time. The first person takes their first choice; nothing in that moment tells them the desk they just took was the only tolerable option left for someone else. The damage lands on whoever has the least flexibility, and which person that is depends entirely on an order nobody agreed to.

This system makes no local decisions at all. It builds the matrix points[person, desk], blanks out every cell that is not in that person’s top … or not in a zone they are allowed to sit in, and solves the resulting rectangular linear assignment problem — the standard operations-research problem of matching two sets one-to-one at minimum cost — with the Jonker–Volgenant algorithm (scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment).

“Solves” has its strict meaning here. Not a heuristic. Not annealing, not “good enough after ten thousand iterations”. The algorithm returns an assignment together with a set of dual prices that certifies it: a short arithmetic check anyone can redo, which proves no other assignment scores higher. It runs in polynomial time, so considering every seating at once costs seconds. For scale, with … desks in this year’s pool and … people on the roster who are not keeping a desk, there are roughly … ways to seat everybody before eligibility and top-… narrow it down. None of them are enumerated. (The two figures on this page do enumerate, because they are small enough that you can check them by hand — that is the point of them.)

The guarantee: nothing outside your top …

You rank … desks. Nobody is assigned a desk outside their own submitted …. This is structural rather than aspirational: a desk you did not rank has no cell in your row of the matrix, so there is no arithmetic that could put you there. It is then asserted again after the solve, in case that sentence ever stops being true.

If the floor cannot be met — if some group of people collectively ranked fewer desks than there are of them — the run fails. Non-zero exit code, no assignment file written. What comes out instead is a diagnostic naming the smallest group that is over-subscribed: 

6 people can only reach 4 desks between them:
  people: ...
  desks:  ...
  short by: 2

…plus a round-2 file listing exactly those people and the desks genuinely still open to them. The coordinator comes back to that group and asks for more ranks. Everyone else’s assignment is deliberately not frozen at that point: publishing it would tell the round-2 people precisely what to avoid, which is an invitation to rank strategically rather than honestly.

The failure mode this rules out is the one that actually destroys trust: quietly handing somebody their ninth choice and hoping they do not notice. A loud failure with names in it is worse for the coordinator and better for everyone else, so that is the trade the system makes.

Ties, and why the seed is published first

Two different seatings can score exactly the same total. Something has to choose. Here is exactly what does, and exactly how much it is capable of.

This year’s tie-break seed … …

It is announced before the form opens. SHA-256 of that string gives an integer; that integer seeds NumPy’s PCG64 generator; that stream does two things, and only two:

  • It shuffles the rows and columns before solving and un-shuffles after, so the answer can never depend on where you happen to land in an alphabetised roster.
  • It adds a tiny nudge to every cell of the points matrix.

The nudge is the part that sounds alarming, so here is the bound. Curve values are converted to exact integers before anything else happens, which means any two achievable totals differ by at least 1. Each nudge is drawn from [0, ε) with ε = 1 / (2(n+1)) for n people, so the total nudge across a whole seating is strictly less than ½. Half a point cannot overturn a gap of a whole point. The nudge is therefore able to choose among assignments that are exactly tied, and is arithmetically incapable of anything else. The solver re-checks n·ε < 1 against the actual matrix at runtime, and separately verifies that the un-nudged total of the answer it returns equals the un-nudged optimum.

Plainly: the seed cannot make you lose a contest you won. It can only settle a genuine draw.

Why not break ties alphabetically, or by year, or by seniority? Because that is not a tie-break, it is a standing advantage. “Earliest surname wins every draw” means the same handful of people win every draw, this year and every year after, and the effect compounds because those are the same people every time. A published random seed spreads draws around; a name does not. Publishing it in advance is what makes the claim checkable rather than a promise: anyone can re-run the solver with the announced seed and the published hash of the response file and get the same result, and re-running with a different seed produces a visibly different output hash.

Why some desks are not offered to you

The room is divided into zones, and not every zone is open to everyone. This is a rule table in eligibility.json, evaluated top to bottom, first match wins — not a branch buried in the code.     An ineligible desk is not scored low — it is absent from your row of the matrix. No amount of total-satisfaction arithmetic can seat you in one, and the server re-checks it when you submit rather than trusting this page.

    What this does not do

    The objective is a sum, so it maximises the total and not the worst individual outcome: it will accept one person landing further down their list if that buys two other people their first choice, which is a real value judgement and not a mathematical necessity — the published report prints the full rank histogram so you can see exactly when that happened. The shape of the curve is likewise a departmental choice: a steeper one protects first choices harder at the cost of the tail, so the report re-runs the identical responses under the comparison curves in scoring.json and shows whether the answer was even sensitive to it. Because the curve is fixed and public, the mechanism is not strategy-proof in the formal sense — somebody who already knew everyone else’s list could in principle do better by misreporting theirs — though nobody can see anyone else’s list, and a dishonest ranking mostly injures the person who submitted it. It cannot conjure desks: if demand collapses onto too few of them it fails loudly rather than fudging. And it takes as given the things people actually argue about — who keeps their desk, where the zone divider sits, how many ranks are required — all of which are policy fixed before the form opens, not outputs of the algorithm.
